Wall and Drywall Water Damage in Longview

A sheet of drywall behaves much as a wick does. Once the base of a wall gets wet, moisture climbs through the paper and gypsum, commonly a foot or more higher than the water stood on the floor. Insulation and the rear of the panel can be wet through while the paint out front shows nothing unusual. Looks cannot be trusted here, so every wall gets tested with a meter.

A wet wall does not automatically mean demolition. Firm panels wetted by clean water are frequently saved, in some cases with the baseboard taken off and small openings drilled so air can move inside the wall. If the drywall has gone soft, crumbles, shows mold or took in dirty water, we do a flood cut, meaning a level cut over the damaged area with all material beneath it removed.

Warning Signs

  • Caulk lines cracking or gaps opening where trim meets the wall
  • Wall coverings lifting, blistering or creasing close to floor level
  • Nail or screw heads popping out and seams showing through the paint
  • Soft or crumbly spots you notice when pushing lightly on the wall
  • Fuzzy or dark patches hidden in corners or in back of furniture
  • A tide line or discoloration running along the bottom of the wall
What You Cannot See

The Damage You Do Not See

Mold Inside Cavities

Mold thrives on the rear face of drywall because it is paper covered, dark and has no moving air. You can have a clean looking painted surface with heavy growth right behind it.

Walls You Cannot See

Drywall hidden in back of cabinetry and built-in units tends to be missed and dries very slowly. Checking under the cabinet base and on the reverse face tells us if it is wet.

Wet Sheathing Outside

Outside walls have sheathing past the insulation that can also get soaked. That layer dries slowly and is checked before the wall is closed.

Wet Outlets and Wiring

Outlets and wiring are mounted near the floor, exactly where the water gathers. Leave the outlets on a damp wall alone until someone has checked the circuit.

How We Fix It

How We Handle Wall and Drywall Water Damage

1

Map the Moisture

Every wall is checked with a thermal camera and moisture meter, and we mark the height and reach of the water, covering the other face and the rooms next door.

2

Decide What Stays

Based on the water category, how long it sat and how the drywall feels, we tell you plainly which sections can be dried in place and which need to come out.

3

Cut or Ventilate

For walls being saved, baseboards come off and air is directed into the cavity. Walls that cannot be kept get a flood cut along a level line, with the wet insulation pulled.

4

Dry and Rebuild

Dehumidifiers and air movers stay on, usually 3 to 5 days, until the studs and the drywall left in place test dry. The rebuild follows with fresh insulation, drywall installation and finishing, trim and a coat of paint.

The Clock

The Damage Timeline

First hours

Water Starts Climbing

Moisture enters where the panel meets the floor and wicks upward from there. Baseboards and the bottom plate take on moisture.

24 to 48 hours

Wall Swells

Moisture keeps climbing, the baseboards puff up and blisters form in the paint. Inside the cavity, the paper face becomes a place mold can begin.

Day 3 to 7

Panels Lose Strength

Gypsum softens and the paper lets go. Within the wall the insulation sags, a smell develops and mold moves over the back of the panel.

After a week

Framing Is Affected

Studs and plates stay wet long enough to grow mold and begin to decay. A far larger section of wall needs tearing out and rebuilding.

Matching paint is trickiest because aged paint has faded and touch ups stand out. So the standard approach is painting the full wall, one corner to the other.
Look low on the wall first. Common clues are baseboards that have swelled, paint that bubbles or peels, a pale water mark and a musty odor. The wall may also feel cool or slightly soft. How far it spread is something these clues cannot reveal. Reading behind the surface requires a moisture meter and, in some cases, a thermal camera.
